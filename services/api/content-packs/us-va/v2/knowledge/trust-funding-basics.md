# Funding your trust

Funding is the step that makes a trust real: transferring ownership of
your assets from your individual name into the name of the trust. A
signed trust document with no assets in it controls nothing, and assets
left outside the trust may still have to go through probate.

Common funding steps, by asset type:

- **Real estate**: a new deed is prepared, signed, and recorded with the
  clerk of the circuit court in the city or county where the property
  sits, transferring the property to the trustee.
- **Bank and brokerage accounts**: the financial institution retitles the
  account to the trustee, or is given forms naming the trust. Each
  institution has its own paperwork.
- **Business interests**: ownership interests are assigned to the trust
  using the company's own transfer rules.
- **Personal property**: items without title documents (furniture,
  jewelry, household goods) are usually transferred with a written
  assignment.

Some assets are normally NOT moved into a revocable trust. Retirement
accounts such as IRAs and 401(k)s keep their tax treatment only while
owned by an individual, so they stay in your name — the trust may be
involved through beneficiary designations instead, which has tax
consequences your attorney should review first. Life insurance and
payable-on-death designations also pass by their own forms.

Funding is not a one-time event. When you buy a new home, open a new
account, or start a business after creating the trust, ask whether the
new asset should be titled to the trust so it stays inside the plan.

This guide is educational only and is not legal advice. Your attorney can
confirm which of your assets belong in the trust and review the transfers.
