# Digital assets and accounts

Your digital life — email, photos, cloud storage, social media,
financial apps, cryptocurrency, domain names, loyalty points — is part
of what you leave behind, and it follows different rules than physical
property.

Virginia has adopted a law (the Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital
Assets Act) that sets a priority order for who can access your accounts
after death or incapacity:

1. **The service's own tool**, if you used one — for example, a legacy
   contact or inactive-account setting offered by the provider. This
   choice overrides everything else.
2. **Your estate documents**, if they grant your executor, trustee, or
   agent access.
3. **The service's terms of use**, which often restrict or deny access
   when neither of the above speaks.

Practical steps that make this manageable for your family:

- Name the people in your documents: give your executor and trustee
  express authority over digital assets if you want them to have it.
- Use the built-in legacy tools on major accounts where they exist, and
  keep those choices consistent with your documents.
- Keep an inventory — which services, what is stored there, roughly what
  it is worth — stored securely with your plan documents. Never put
  passwords in your will: wills can become public records in probate.
  A password manager's emergency-access feature is a safer route.
- Remember cryptocurrency is self-custodied: without keys or recovery
  phrases, it is usually gone for good, so its access plan matters more
  than most.

This guide is educational only and is not legal advice.
