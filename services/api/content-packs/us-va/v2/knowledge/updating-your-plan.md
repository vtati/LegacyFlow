# Keeping your plan up to date

An estate plan is a snapshot. When the picture changes, the plan should
too. The events that most often call for a review:

- **Marriage or divorce.** Marriage adds a person the plan should
  address. Divorce is the opposite emergency: Virginia law generally
  treats a former spouse as having died before you for purposes of your
  will after a divorce, unless the will says otherwise — but beneficiary
  designations on insurance and retirement accounts do not all follow
  that rule, and must be updated by hand.
- **Children.** A birth or adoption, a child reaching adulthood, or a
  child developing needs the plan did not anticipate.
- **Deaths.** A beneficiary, executor, trustee, guardian, or agent who
  has died (or can no longer serve) leaves a hole in the plan.
- **Moves.** Moving to another state does not usually invalidate a plan,
  but the new state has different rules about execution, property, and
  probate — a review after a move is money well spent.
- **Money changes.** Buying or selling a home or business, a large
  inheritance, or a big change in what you own — and for trust owners,
  remembering to title new assets to the trust.
- **Law changes.** Tax and estate laws change on both the federal and
  state level; a plan written under old law may waste opportunities or
  create problems.

How changes are made matters. A will is changed with a signed, witnessed
amendment (a codicil) or a new will — never by writing on the original.
A revocable trust is changed by a written amendment, or restated
entirely when changes are extensive. Powers of attorney and directives
are usually replaced with fresh documents, and the old ones should be
revoked clearly so no one relies on them.

As a rhythm, reviewing your plan every three to five years — and after
any event above — keeps it aligned with your life. This guide is
educational only and is not legal advice.
