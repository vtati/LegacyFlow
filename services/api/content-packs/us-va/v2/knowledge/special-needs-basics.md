# Planning for a beneficiary with special needs

Leaving money directly to a person who receives means-tested public
benefits — such as Supplemental Security Income (SSI) or Medicaid —
can do harm instead of good. Those programs limit how much a person may
own; an inheritance, even a modest one, can suspend or end benefits the
person depends on for housing, healthcare, and care.

The standard planning tool is a **special needs trust** (also called a
supplemental needs trust):

- Assets are held by a trustee for the beneficiary, not owned by the
  beneficiary, so they generally do not count against benefit limits.
- The trustee uses the money to supplement — not replace — what the
  benefits provide: things like travel, technology, therapies or
  services the programs do not cover, and quality-of-life extras.
- The trustee must never distribute in ways that duplicate benefits or
  hand the beneficiary countable cash; the rules are technical, and
  administration matters as much as drafting.

Common forms include a third-party trust (created by parents or other
relatives, the usual estate-planning route), a first-party trust
(funded with the beneficiary's own money, such as a lawsuit settlement,
with payback rules at death), and pooled trusts run by nonprofits.

Because benefit programs differ by state and change over time, and
because a drafting or administration mistake can cost the beneficiary
their benefits, this area always requires an attorney who works in it —
treat any standard will-or-trust beneficiary clause naming a person
with a disability as a warning sign to get specialist review.
