# What a will does

A will (last will and testament) is the document that states who receives
your property when you die, who will manage that process, and — if you have
minor children — who you want to raise them.

A will generally lets you:

- Name the people and organizations that receive your assets.
- Name an executor: the person who carries out the will's instructions
  under court supervision.
- Nominate a guardian for minor children.
- Create small trusts inside the will itself (for example, to hold a
  child's inheritance until they are older).

A will does not control everything. Assets with their own beneficiary
designations — life insurance, retirement accounts, payable-on-death bank
accounts — usually pass by those designations instead, no matter what the
will says. Property owned jointly with right of survivorship also passes
to the surviving owner.

A will only takes effect at death, and it generally must go through
probate (the court-supervised process described in the probate guide)
before assets are distributed. Until you die, you can change your will at
any time as long as you are legally competent, using the same signing
formalities as the original.

This guide is educational only and is not legal advice. Your attorney
finalizes your will as part of your plan documents.
