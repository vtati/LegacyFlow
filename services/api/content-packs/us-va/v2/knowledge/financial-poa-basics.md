# Financial power of attorney

A financial power of attorney (POA) is a document in which you — the
principal — name an agent to handle money and property matters for you:
paying bills, managing bank accounts, dealing with real estate, and
similar tasks.

Virginia's Uniform Power of Attorney Act governs these documents. Points
that matter when planning:

- **Durable by default.** In Virginia, a POA remains effective if you
  later become incapacitated, unless the document says otherwise. That
  durability is usually the point: the agent can act exactly when you
  cannot.
- **You choose the powers.** A POA can be broad or limited to specific
  tasks. Certain powerful authorities — such as making gifts, changing
  beneficiary designations, or creating trusts — must be granted
  expressly; they are never implied.
- **Successors and co-agents.** You can name a backup agent if the first
  cannot serve, and decide whether multiple agents act together or
  independently.
- **The agent is a fiduciary.** An agent must act in your best interest,
  keep your money separate, and keep records of what they do.
- **It ends at death.** A POA gives no authority after the principal
  dies; from that point the will or trust governs.

A POA only works if third parties accept it. Virginia law expects banks
and others to accept a valid POA and provides consequences for
unreasonable refusals, but institutions may still ask for their own
review, so families should not wait for a crisis to discover a problem.

A financial POA covers money and property. Medical decisions are covered
by a separate document, the advance medical directive. This guide is
educational only and is not legal advice.
