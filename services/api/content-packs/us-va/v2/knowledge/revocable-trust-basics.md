# Revocable living trusts

A revocable living trust is a legal arrangement you create during your
lifetime. You transfer ownership of assets into the trust, and the trustee
manages them under the rules you wrote. Most people name themselves as the
first trustee, so day-to-day control of the assets does not change.

Key terms:

- **Grantor** (or settlor): the person who creates and funds the trust.
- **Trustee**: the person or institution that manages trust assets.
- **Successor trustee**: the person who steps in when the first trustee
  dies or cannot act.
- **Beneficiaries**: the people who receive the trust's benefits.

Because the trust is revocable, you can amend it or cancel it entirely at
any time while you are competent. Under Virginia's Uniform Trust Code, a
trust is generally revocable unless its terms say it is irrevocable.

People use revocable trusts to:

- Let a successor trustee manage assets immediately if they become
  incapacitated, without a court-appointed guardian for those assets.
- Pass assets at death without those assets going through probate.
- Control how and when beneficiaries receive their inheritance — for
  example, in stages at certain ages, or held for a beneficiary who needs
  support managing money.

A trust only controls assets that are actually transferred into it. That
transfer step is called funding, and it is essential: an unfunded trust
controls nothing. Most trust plans also include a pour-over will, which
acts as a safety net that sends leftover assets into the trust at death
(after probate for those assets).

A revocable living trust does not, by itself, reduce estate taxes or
protect assets from your own creditors. This guide is educational only
and is not legal advice.
