# If you die without a will

When a Virginia resident dies without a valid will, they are said to die
intestate, and Virginia law — not the family — decides who receives the
estate. The law follows a fixed order of relatives called the course of
descents.

In general terms, for someone who dies intestate in Virginia:

- If there is a surviving spouse and all of the children are also the
  spouse's children, the spouse receives the whole estate.
- If any child is not the surviving spouse's child, the spouse receives
  one-third and the children share the other two-thirds.
- With no surviving spouse, the estate passes to children, then to
  parents, then to siblings and their descendants, and on through more
  distant relatives in a set order.

Intestacy has practical consequences families often do not expect:

- The court chooses the administrator of the estate and the guardian of
  minor children, guided by the statute's preferences — not by your
  wishes, because you left none in writing.
- An unmarried partner receives nothing under intestacy law.
- A child's inheritance may arrive outright at age 18, with no one
  managing it for them.
- Blended families are the most common source of surprises, because the
  spouse-versus-children split applies whether or not it matches what the
  family would have chosen.

Dying without a will does not avoid probate — the estate is still
administered through the court process. A will replaces the state's
default plan with your own choices. This guide is educational only and
is not legal advice.
