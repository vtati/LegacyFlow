1 · The big picture

LegacyFlow's data falls into seven neighborhoods. A firm serves households; each household's estate plan is a matter; the AI interview gathers the facts that become confirmed answers; documents are generated from those answers; the firm's attorneys review and approve them; and a platform layer — jurisdictions, content packs, audit trail, email — holds the whole thing up. Almost every table carries a firm_id and/or a matter_id: those two columns are the twin spines of the schema, and they are what make firm-by-firm isolation possible.

Accounts, Firms & Access

Who is allowed in, and in what role. Every person who signs in gets a profile; law firms are tenants of the platform, and the people who work for a firm are connected to it through memberships with roles. Attorneys arrive through applications and invitations, raise their hand for cases through case requests, and get paid through payouts.

Households, People & Property

The family side of the world. A household is the family unit a firm serves; inside it live person records (clients, spouses, children, guardians — people who may never sign in themselves), the relationships between them, and the family's balance sheet: what they own (assets) and what they owe (liabilities).

Two reading tips. First, a foreign key (marked FK → in the cards) is just a column whose value is another row's ID — it is how “this document belongs to that matter” is written down. Second, most tables are never deleted from: history tables (status history, audit events) only ever grow, so the past can always be reconstructed.

4 · Follow the rows

Four everyday stories, told as the database sees them — which rows appear, where, and in what order.

A family signs up and starts a plan

  1. A login is born. Registering creates the Auth record, and a trigger immediately adds a matching profiles row — the person's identity inside the app.
  2. The household appears. Choosing Virginia at signup creates a households row with origin = self_serve. The platform looks up jurisdiction_default_firms for US-VA to learn which firm this family belongs to.
  3. People take their places. A household_members row makes the signer the household's primary_client, and a persons row records them as a human in the plan — two different facts, deliberately kept apart.
  4. The plan is created. Starting a plan writes the matters row: household, firm, jurisdiction, and the exact jurisdiction_packs version pinned to it, plus a frozen jurisdiction_snapshot. The first matter_status_history entry and audit_events rows record the birth of the plan.

An interview answer travels

  1. The conversation. Each message becomes an interview_turns row (summary, sequence number) with its full text stored encrypted in interview_turn_bodies. Every model call leaves an ai_traces row.
  2. Facts are proposed, not written. Facts the AI pulls from the conversation — or from a document logged in interview_extractions — land in field_proposals with a source and a confidence score.
  3. The family decides. Confirming a proposal marks it confirmed; the value is written into intake_answers, the one canonical answer row for that question.
  4. People sync from answers. Save-time syncs reconcile answers into persons and relationships rows (this is how the spouse's name reaches the Will), and role answers become nominations. Periodically, a Consistency Reviewer run (interview_consistency_reviews) re-checks everything and raises escalations for anything that contradicts itself.

A submitted plan becomes signed documents

  1. Submit freezes the plan. Submitting writes a review_packets snapshot — the plan exactly as the attorney will see it — and the matter's status moves to ATTORNEY_REVIEW in matter_status_history.
  2. Gates are decided. approval_gates rows record the intake approval and, later, the document-release decision: who approved, when, with what notes.
  3. Documents are generated. For each approved document_templates entry, a document_packages row is generated from the answers, stamped with their source_fingerprint. Each render adds a document_versions row pointing at a file_uploads record — watermarked while it is a draft, clean once released.
  4. Signing and funding. Release produces the signing_checklists row that walks the family through execution; afterwards, funding_tasks track moving assets into the trust, with evidence files, until the matter reaches PLAN_IMPLEMENTED.

The Attorney Reviewer writes findings

  1. A run begins. Generating or submitting documents starts an attorney_review_runs row keyed to the answers' source_fingerprint — if the answers haven't changed, the review hasn't either.
  2. The evidence is kept. The exact payload the reviewer read is stored in attorney_review_sources, and the rendered text of each document in attorney_review_snapshots — a finding can always be audited against the precise words.
  3. Findings land. Deterministic checks and the AI layer write attorney_review_findings, each with severity should_fix or question_for_attorney. The reviewer is advisory: findings never block the release gate.
  4. Acknowledgment sticks. When an attorney accepts a finding, the finding's disposition becomes acknowledged with their name and note. Regenerate the documents and the same issue keeps the same finding_hash — so the acknowledgment follows it instead of resurfacing as “new”.

Row-Level Security (RLS)

RLS — the database's own per-row access control — is switched on for all 56 tables. 45 tables carry 135 access policies between them; the remaining 11 (the Attorney Reviewer tables, attorney applications, case requests, payouts, interview parked items, the default-firm routing table, the worker dead-letter inbox, and the Ask knowledge index) are deliberately policy-free with RLS on, which means direct database/API-role access is denied by default and only the application backend can reach them. One honest architectural note: tenant isolation between firms is additionally enforced in the application code itself — the API checks firm scope on every request — which is a documented, deliberate posture for this system.

Files live outside the tables

The tables above are the index, not the warehouse. File bytes live in three private storage buckets — client-uploads, plan-documents, and tmp-seed — and file_uploads rows point at them. Login accounts themselves live in Supabase Auth (auth.users); profiles.id is a foreign key to it, which is the one link that leaves the public schema.
